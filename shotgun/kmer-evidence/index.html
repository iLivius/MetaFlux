    Species-level k-mer evidence¶

    Read this after Choosing confidence and threshold

    Bracken's depth-scaled read threshold, described on that page, has the largest effect on precision in shotgun mode; set it first. This gate catches one narrow kind of error that the threshold cannot detect. It is off by default; see Why the default is enabled: false.

    Kraken2's read count tells you how many reads it chose to label with a taxon. It does not tell you how much sequence unique to that organism actually turned up in the sample — and those are different questions.

    • A species that is genuinely present contributes many different species-specific sequences.
    • A spurious call is usually a small set of sequences — conserved, repetitive, or shared with a relative that really is abundant — matched over and over.

    In a read count the two look identical. Running Kraken2 with --report-minimizer-data adds the number that separates them. MetaFlux always does.

    The two extra columns¶

    The report becomes eight columns instead of six, with two inserted before the rank code:

    Column Contents
    4 Number of minimizers in the read data associated with this taxon
    5 An estimate of the number of distinct minimizers in the read data associated with this taxon

    Column 5 is an estimate, not an exact count — Kraken2 uses a HyperLogLog sketch rather than storing every minimizer. Both columns are clade-cumulative, like the read counts.

    What "associated with this taxon" actually means

    The columns do not count the minimizers carried by the reads assigned to the taxon.

    Kraken2 attributes each minimizer occurrence to whichever taxon that minimizer maps to in the database (its lowest common ancestor), pooled over every read in the sample, regardless of where each read finally got assigned.

    This is visible in any report: taxa with two or three assigned reads can carry hundreds of thousands of minimizer counts. In SRR39106521, one of the test samples described below, the genus row Streptomyces has 3 clade reads and 343,359 total minimizers, about 500 times the 690 k-mer positions that three pairs of 149-base reads can hold. Counts that high cannot come from the assigned reads alone; they are expected when minimizers are attributed by LCA.

    So column 5 for a species means: how many distinct database minimizers specific to that species were seen anywhere in this sample. That is a statement about how much species-specific sequence is present — the minimizer counterpart of what KrakenUniq calls unique k-mers, so the published thresholds below are a fair starting point; how well they convert to minimizers is discussed below. It is not a genome-coverage measure, and describing it as "how much of the genome the reads covered" would be wrong.

    The flag itself changes no classification

    --report-minimizer-data is purely additive reporting: with or without it, Kraken2 writes a byte-for-byte identical per-read output and the same values in every shared report column.

    Reading the numbers¶

    Two species from SRR39106521, with similar read counts:

    Species Reads Distinct minimizers Total minimizers Duplicity
    Pseudomonas aeruginosa 4,775 68,276 221,570 3.2
    Pigmentiphaga sp. CHJ604 4,129 929 226,726 244.1

    The two also have almost the same total minimizer count. The first is supported by 68,276 distinct species-specific minimizers. The second is supported by 929, each one seen about 240 times.

    Duplicity (total ÷ distinct) is the most directly readable of the three numbers, because it does not require knowing the sequencing depth. It also barely tracks abundance: across the 2,418 species rows in the Kraken2 reports of the four test samples, Pearson r against log reads is +0.17 for log duplicity and +0.71 for the log distinct count. That makes it the better number to eyeball. It is nonetheless a worse filter (see What does not work), so MetaFlux reports it and thresholds the distinct count.

    What MetaFlux writes¶

    Every shotgun run produces these, per sample, whether or not the gate is switched on:

    02.classification/kraken2/{sample}_species_evidence.tsv   one row per taxon at the Bracken rank
02.classification/kraken2/{sample}_report_gated.txt       the report with failing taxa removed
stats/kmer_evidence/{sample}_evidence.png                 reads vs distinct minimizers, log-log

    The gated report is written even when the gate is off, precisely so you can diff it against the Kraken2 report and see what enabling the gate would do to your data before trusting it.

    The evidence table carries, per taxon: taxid, name, rank, clade and direct reads, total and distinct minimizers, duplicity, minimizers per read, percent of classified reads, and the verdict with its reason.

    The gate¶

    shotgun:
  kmer_evidence:
    enabled: false                     # report only; pipeline unchanged
    min_distinct_minimizers: 300       # the floor; see the unit conversion below
    min_reads: 0                       # extra read floor, applied together with the above
    never_prune_above_fraction: 0.01   # safety rail

    Where it acts¶

    kraken2  →  kmer_evidence  →  bracken  →  finalize_otu_table

    The gate sits between Kraken2 and Bracken. It does not replace or skip Bracken — it hands Bracken a cleaned report instead of the raw one. Because the correction happens upstream, it flows all the way through: otu_table.biom and otu_table.tsv become the corrected tables. There is no separate filtered file to reconcile.

    Switching the gate off restores the previous behaviour exactly: Bracken reads Kraken2's own report and nothing downstream changes.

    How a taxon is removed¶

    Two details must be handled correctly; otherwise Bracken redistributes the reads of the pruned report wrongly, without an error.

    Whole subtree, never a bare row. Bracken rebuilds the taxonomy tree from the indentation of the report, attaching each row to the last row one level above it. Delete a species row and leave its strain (S1) children behind, and those children get re-parented onto whichever species happens to precede them, whose read count is then inflated without any warning. So when a taxon fails, everything indented beneath it goes too.

    The reads are credited to the parent, not discarded. A pruned taxon's whole clade total is added to its parent's own-node count, so Bracken redistributes those reads among the siblings that passed. The parent's clade total is untouched, so the report's arithmetic still balances: for every node, clade == direct + sum(children clade), and no column other than a parent's direct count ever changes.

    Two things this does not guarantee

    Not all reads land on close relatives. The rationale for crediting the parent is that a spurious call usually borrows evidence from a neighbour. That holds for most of the reads that move, not all: part of them cross genus or family boundaries.

    Some reads are still lost. If every species under a genus fails, Bracken has nothing left at the target rank to redistribute to, and drops those reads, usually a small fraction of the sample. Crediting the parent is much better than deleting rows outright, but it is not conservation.

    Choosing a threshold¶

    Three published sources give thresholds for this kind of filter, all expressed in KrakenUniq's unique k-mers:

    Source What it reports
    KrakenUniq (Breitwieser, Baker & Salzberg, 2018) On a synthetic mix of reads from bacterial isolates (1–34 million read pairs), the best threshold (highest recall at a false discovery rate of 5 % or less) rose by about 2,000 unique k-mers per million reads sequenced: about 3,700 at 1 million, 21,000 at 10 million. For ten patient biopsies, mostly host DNA, a fixed 1,000 unique k-mers together with at least 10 reads removed the background calls and kept every confirmed pathogen.
    Oskolkov (2026), Front. Microbiol. 17:1603339 Simulated ancient metagenomes (fragments of about 42 bases, 0.1–0.7 million reads): about 1,000 unique k-mers gave the best F1, and a read-count filter added nothing. The best value rose with depth, by about 200 unique k-mers per 100,000 reads, a trend the paper calls relatively weak. Run on the same data, Kraken2 needed two to three times more distinct minimizers than KrakenUniq needed unique k-mers.
    aMeta (Pochon et al., 2023) Defaults of 1,000 unique k-mers and 200 assigned reads, set from more than 1,200 ancient libraries; users are invited to try 500–1,500 k-mers depending on depth. The 2026 study above comes from the same group and reuses aMeta's simulated data.

    Published thresholds count k-mers; Kraken2 reports minimizers

    In plain terms. Kraken2 reads a sequence as overlapping 35-base k-mers, but it does not look each one up. For every k-mer it picks one 31-base piece inside it, the minimizer, and stores and counts only that. Neighbouring k-mers usually pick the same piece, so a region of a genome gives about three times fewer distinct minimizers than distinct k-mers. The studies above counted unique k-mers and settled on about 1,000. In a Kraken2 report the same amount of sequence shows up as about 333 distinct minimizers. The default floor, 300, is that figure rounded down, because the conversion is only approximate. It was not tuned on MetaFlux's own test data, and it is a low, cautious value rather than an exact equivalent. When Oskolkov (2026) ran both tools on the same simulated data, the best Kraken2 floor was two to three times higher than the best KrakenUniq one, where the conversion predicts three times lower, and MetaFlux's own benchmark finds useful floors in the thousands (see Scaling the floor with depth).

    The arithmetic, for a database built with other settings. How many k-mers share a minimizer depends on the database's k and l. Each k-mer of length k spans w = k − l + 1 candidate minimizer positions of length l, and the standard expected density of selected minimizers is 2 / (w + 1). For the usual nucleotide settings (k = 35, l = 31) that is w = 5 and a density of 1/3 — roughly one distinct minimizer per three k-mers.

    So, counting sequence alone, a published 1,000 unique k-mers corresponds to about 333 distinct minimizers here.

    Check k and l on the first line of your database's inspect.txt and recompute if they differ. (The 2/(w+1) density is the expectation for a random minimizer scheme; Kraken2's spaced-seed masking shifts it slightly, so treat 333 as an approximate conversion, not an exact one.)

    Scaling the floor with depth¶

    A species that is really present leaves more distinct minimizers the deeper a sample is sequenced, and so, up to a limit, does a spurious call that borrows sequence from an abundant relative. A fixed floor is therefore lenient on deep samples and strict on shallow ones. KrakenUniq and Oskolkov (2026) reported the same for unique k-mers (table above), but neither gives a depth rule for Kraken2.

    MetaFlux's benchmark was used to fit one. It covers 42 samples of known composition: one real mock community (ZymoBIOMICS D6331, SRA run SRR38976715, BioProject PRJNA1474435) and 41 simulated CAMI II samples from the marine, rhizosphere and strain-madness sets (see Benchmark data), with 2.6–6.5 million classified read pairs each, classified with three Kraken2 indices: k2_pluspf_20260626 (at confidence 0.15 and 0.20), k2_gtdb_genome_reps_20250609 (GTDB r226 representative genomes) and k2_NCBI_reference_20251007 (one genome per NCBI species). A floor of one distinct minimizer per 1,000 classified read pairs gave the best result that was safe everywhere:

    • species-level F1 rose by 0.066 on average (0.036 with PlusPF), against 0.0020 for the fixed default of 300 (0.0003 with PlusPF);
    • it removed 2,288 of 13,115 false species calls and 6 of 13,419 true ones (none with PlusPF), and lowered no sample's F1;
    • it stayed safe when shallower sequencing was modelled, down to 1 % of the reads, while every fixed floor tested, from 300 to 10,000, lowered some samples' F1 at some depth.

    This is the same order of magnitude as KrakenUniq's slope (about 670 distinct minimizers per million read pairs once converted), but the number itself comes from MetaFlux's benchmark. As a starting point:

    Classified read pairs in the shallowest sample, host excluded min_distinct_minimizers
    500,000 500
    1 million 1,000
    2 million 2,000
    5 million 5,000
    6.5 million or more 6,500

    Each sample's classified count is in stats/read_tracking.txt (column classified) and in its Bracken log ([bracken] auto threshold: 5e-05 x N classified read pairs). MetaFlux applies one floor to every sample in a run, so size it on the shallowest sample; the deeper samples then get a more lenient floor than they could take. Below about 500,000 classified pairs the scaled floor gains little. Use it even when it comes out lower than the default of 300: in benchmark samples thinned to 26,000–195,000 classified pairs, the fixed floor of 300 lowered some samples' F1 and the scaled floor lowered none.

    Limits of this rule:

    • It rests on limited data. Three quarters of the F1 gain came from the one real mock community; on the simulated CAMI samples it was 0.01–0.03. The shallower depths were modelled from the observed counts, not sequenced.
    • Do not go above 1.5 per 1,000 pairs. At that rate rare true species start to be removed (92 of 13,419 true calls), and from 2 per 1,000 some samples' F1 falls even at full depth.
    • Do not go above 6,500 for deeper samples. The benchmark stops at 6.5 million classified pairs. A floor that keeps growing with depth eventually exceeds what a real species with a small species-specific part of its genome can reach; in the model this began at about 10 million pairs.
    • Exclude host reads. PlusPF contains the human genome and host removal is off by default, so host reads would raise the floor without adding any microbial evidence. Subtract the read pairs Kraken2 assigned to the host (for human, the Homo sapiens row of the report), or remove the host first (Decontamination).
    • Library layout. The rule assumes paired 2×150 bp Illumina reads with little mate overlap and few duplicates. Shorter reads, single-end data, overlapping mates and duplicate-rich libraries give fewer distinct minimizers per read pair, so the same floor is stricter for them; use a lower value.
    • It changes the table more than 300 does. On the test dataset above (1.7–3.5 million classified pairs), a floor of 1,673, sized on the shallowest sample with its host reads excluded, keeps 33–68 species per sample, against 66–104 at 300, and the species it removes hold 10–33 % of the read pairs, which move to relatives. That is still well above the 9–16 genomes sylph finds. Compare otu_table.tsv with the gate off and on before relying on it.

    Why the default is enabled: false¶

    The gate is off by default because a correctly scaled Bracken threshold already removes most of what it would remove. It has been run on 46 samples: the D6331 mock, the four PRJNA1477390 metagenomes and 41 CAMI II samples. In gate-on/gate-off runs of CAMI II samples at several depths with PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015, the gate changed species-level F1 by a mean of +0.0009 at the depth-scaled threshold, and by zero on the strain-madness and rhizosphere samples at every depth tested. At the former fixed -t 10 the mean was +0.0079, about nine times more. So the gate mainly helps when the Bracken threshold is not scaled to depth; with a scaled threshold it adds little.

    Which database build those two numbers come from

    Both figures come from gate-on/gate-off runs on PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015 (15 sample-depth combinations at the scaled threshold, 11 at the fixed one). The k2_pluspf_20260626 benchmark runs had the gate off; removing from their Bracken output the species the gate would prune at the default floor of 300 changes mean species-level F1 by +0.0003.

    Unchanged F1 does not mean an unchanged table¶

    The two filters ask different questions. The threshold asks how many reads; the gate asks how much distinct sequence. A taxon can pass one and fail the other, and an aggregate score of 250+ species barely notices when a single one flips.

    Pseudomonas sp. JS425 in the four test samples, with the Bracken threshold scaled to depth (threshold_alpha 5×10⁻⁵):

    Sample Classified pairs Bracken -t Kraken2 pairs Distinct minimizers Passes -t After Bracken, gate off Gate
    SRR39106521 2,268,774 113 372 182 yes 69,992 removed
    SRR39106522 3,458,847 173 103 181 no – removed
    SRR39106523 1,673,810 84 301 181 yes 51,418 removed
    SRR39106524 2,177,429 109 123 181 yes 8,935 removed

    In SRR39106521 the 372 read pairs pass the threshold, but they rest on 182 distinct minimizers, each hit about 60 times, and Bracken raises the call to 3 % of the sample. The threshold keeps JS425 in three samples and drops it from the fourth only because the cut-off falls differently there; in a presence/absence plot that would look like a real difference between samples. The gate removes it from all four.

    These samples have no known composition, so they do not enter the F1 scores above. That is why the benchmark shows almost no gain from the gate while the table changes.

    When to consider turning it on: communities the database covers poorly, where reads from organisms absent from the database are assigned to their relatives. The evidence for this is limited. It rests on cases like JS425 in the wastewater test samples, which have no known composition; on the CAMI II rhizosphere samples, the poorly covered benchmark set with a known composition, the gate hardly changed F1. For gut, clinical or other samples close to RefSeq, leave it off. Either way the gated report is written even when the gate is off, so compare it with the Kraken2 report before deciding.

    Validation status¶

    What the gate was tested against, and what those tests could and could not show.

    What has been checked¶

    The gate was checked on one real mock community (Zymo D6331, below) and on CAMI II's marine, rhizosphere and strain-madness gold-standard sets (41 samples): in gate-on/gate-off runs with PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015, and by applying it to the Bracken calls of the k2_pluspf_20260626 runs. These results were scored with the benchmark's own scorer. On the two k2_pluspf_20260626 runs (Kraken2 confidence 0.15 and 0.20) that scorer was checked against OPAL 1.0.13, the CAMI assessment tool: given the same input preparation, OPAL's species-level counts of true positives, false positives and false negatives equal the scorer's in every sample. The full methodology and the numbers that matter for choosing a threshold are on the confidence and threshold page. The result for this gate: once the Bracken threshold is scaled to depth it adds at most 0.002 F1 (PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015), but it still removes calls like JS425 above, which a read-count threshold cannot detect.

    One real mock community: the ZymoBIOMICS Gut Microbiome Standard (D6331), SRA run SRR38976715 (BioProject PRJNA1474435; see Benchmark data), with about 5.3 million classified read pairs.

    The standard is sold as 21 strains. A species-level profiler can only ever see 17 species, because five of the 21 are Escherichia coli strains at 2.8% each that collapse into one E. coli row at 14%. That ceiling is arithmetic, not a reference-database gap. Zymo lists the standard's Fusobacterium as F. nucleatum (strain 2/1/50A), but sylph (GTDB r232), MetaPhlAn 4 and Kraken2 with GTDB r226 or PlusPF k2_pluspf_20260626 place most of these reads in F. animalis, a former subspecies of F. nucleatum now ranked as a species. It is therefore scored as F. animalis, and calls of F. nucleatum, F. polymorphum and F. vincentii count as false positives. Homo sapiens, from human DNA in the sample, is not scored.

    The run was processed twice through the pipeline with PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015, Kraken2 confidence 0.15 and Bracken at the former fixed -t 10: once with the gate off and once with it on at a floor of 333. A floor of 300 gives the same species list on this run. At the current default threshold (-t 265 for this run), only 2 of the 34 false positives in the table below have enough reads to pass (F. nucleatum and F. polymorphum), as does the unscored Homo sapiens; the gate removes none of them, and the threshold itself drops Salmonella enterica.

    Gate off Gate on
    True members recovered 15 / 17 15 / 17
    False-positive species 34 22
    Precision 0.306 0.405
    F1 0.455 0.556
    • No true member was removed. Salmonella enterica, 0.01% of the standard and 134 classified reads, has 3,547 distinct minimizers at a duplicity of 1.5, more than ten times the floor, and is kept. The gate depends on this difference: a rare species can still have many distinct minimizers.
    • The two members never recovered (Enterococcus faecalis at 0.001%, Clostridium perfringens at 0.0001%) have one Kraken2 read each, below Bracken's -t 10, so neither reaches the table in either run. Both have species entries in the database, so they were missed for lack of reads.
    • Quantitative accuracy did not change. With the human row left out, L1 distance to the theoretical composition was 47.15 without the gate and 47.14 with it, Shannon H 2.145 and 2.144, and the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity between the two profiles 0.00009. The 82 taxa the gate removed at 333 held 426 of the 5.3 million reads (81 taxa and 423 reads at 300). Any ordination or differential-abundance test would give the same answer from both tables.
    • Richness, on the other hand, improved substantially — which is where the gate did its work on this dataset:
    Observed richness Truth Gate off Gate on
    Species 17 49 (+188%) 37 (+118%)
    Genera 17 25 (+47%) 18 (+6%)

    On this mock the gate changed which species are listed, not their abundances. If you report how many taxa you detected, or work with presence/absence, that matters. If you only ever compare relative abundances between samples, on data like this it will not change your conclusions.

    Why richness is still over-estimated with the gate on¶

    With the gate on, 37 species other than human are still reported against 17 in the standard. Most of the 22 false positives are not the kind of call the gate is designed to remove.

    Of the 22 false-positive species that survive, 19 are congeners of a genuine member — and the other three are defensible. The table also lists Homo sapiens, which is not scored:

    Survivor Reads after Bracken Distinct minimizers What it is
    Fusobacterium nucleatum, F. vincentii, F. polymorphum 180,835 947–29,678 Close relatives of the true member, F. animalis; all four were subspecies of F. nucleatum until F. animalis, F. vincentii and F. polymorphum were raised to species rank. With k2_pluspf_20251015, Kraken2 gives F. nucleatum more reads than F. animalis (16,709 against 6,062)
    Homo sapiens 965 10,062 Trace human DNA from handling; present in the sample, a contaminant that is not scored
    Shigella dysenteriae 839 1,117 Shigella sits phylogenetically inside E. coli; the genomes are near-identical
    Sphingomonas paucimobilis, Cutibacterium acnes 51 464–779 Common extraction-kit and skin contaminants; plausibly present in the sample
    16 further congeners of Veillonella, Fusobacterium, Saccharomyces, Escherichia, Bacteroides, Candida, Clostridioides 1,874 400–5,380 Close relatives of an abundant true member

    All these survivors carry large distinct-minimizer counts — Saccharomyces paradoxus has 5,380 on just 73 reads, at a duplicity of 1.7. That is broad, un-repetitive evidence, and it is genuinely in the read data: S. cerevisiae is a real member of the standard, and its sequence matches a great deal of what the database files under its sister species.

    So there are two different ways a species-level call goes wrong, and only one is an evidence problem:

    • Thin evidence — a few conserved minimizers hit over and over. Low distinct count, high duplicity. The gate removes these.
    • Mis-attributed evidence — a genuinely present organism whose sequence matches a close relative's database entry. High distinct count, low duplicity. The gate does not remove these, and should not: the sequence is in the sample, and only the species label is wrong.

    The residual inflation is almost entirely the second kind, which is a property of how finely the reference database splits closely related organisms — not something any k-mer counting method can detect. The practical consequence is visible in the table above: at genus rank, where those distinctions disappear, the gate brings richness from +47% to +6% of the true value.

    What this does not show¶

    On the mock the gate removed no true species; on CAMI II it changed F1 very little

    Every organism in D6331 has a species-level entry in the database used. When that is true, classification is already close to correct, most false positives are a thin tail of taxa with few reads, and there is little for an evidence filter to repair, which is what the CAMI II benchmark found: with the Bracken threshold scaled to depth, this gate adds at most 0.002 F1 across marine, rhizosphere and strain-madness samples (PlusPF k2_pluspf_20251015).

    The gate is still useful in cases like JS425 above: a taxon with an ordinary read count but few distinct minimizers. A read-count threshold cannot detect this, however it is scaled.

    The CAMI II benchmark described on the confidence and threshold page covers what one mock cannot: larger and more even communities (marine, 256–381 species per sample), several samples at several depths, communities with genomes absent from the reference (rhizosphere: only 165 of its 307 gold-standard species, 54 %, are in PlusPF k2_pluspf_20260626), and sample types beyond gut and wastewater. It agrees with the D6331 result that the gate removes no true species at full sequencing depth, and it shows that once the Bracken threshold is scaled to depth the gate changes F1 very little.

    Still untested: everything above used short-read Illumina data. Long-read or hybrid data, and sample types with sparser reference coverage than rhizosphere, have not been tried. If you switch the gate on, check the evidence tables for what it removed.

    Limitation: very close relatives¶

    Very close relatives defeat the method, and no k-mer evidence measure fixes this. Shigella dysenteriae is not in D6331, but 13 reads were assigned to it — carrying 1,117 distinct minimizers, so it passes the gate, and Bracken raised it to 839 reads (834 with the gate off). Shigella and E. coli are similar enough that minimizers the database marks as Shigella-specific are genuinely present in E. coli reads. That is a property of the reference taxonomy, not something this filter can detect.

    Threshold stability¶

    A fixed threshold should give a species the same verdict in every sample, but it does not always. Of 620 species found in more than one of the four PRJNA1477390 test samples, 123 (20 %) got different verdicts at the floor of 300:

    • 62 % differ mainly in depth: the species had at least five times fewer reads in one sample, and with few reads it cannot reach enough distinct minimizers. This change of verdict is probably correct.
    • 38 % have read counts less than five-fold apart. 20 of these 47 species lie close to the floor, between 200 and 450 distinct minimizers, in every sample. Sphingomonas paucimobilis is one: 155 to 199 read pairs in each sample, 350 distinct minimizers in SRR39106524 (kept) and 277 to 295 in the other three (removed). The other 27 lie further from the floor in at least one sample, sometimes much further: Sphingopyxis lindanitolerans has 93 to 105 distinct minimizers in three samples and 10,626 in the fourth, on 95 to 282 read pairs.

    Any fixed cut-off behaves like this, whatever its value. Check the evidence tables for species whose verdict differs between samples.

    What does not work¶

    Three approaches were tested on the four PRJNA1477390 test samples and rejected. They are described here because each seems reasonable before it is measured.

    A threshold on distinct minimizers alone is partly an abundance filter. The distinct count correlates with read count (Pearson r = +0.71 on log-log across the 2,418 species rows of the four Kraken2 reports), so some of what it removes, it removes for being rare. This is why the published thresholds pair the k-mer count with an explicit read floor — min_reads exists for exactly that, and is 0 by default only because Oskolkov (2026) found that filtering on unique k-mers alone was enough to reach the best F1, with no read-count filter needed.

    Normalising to minimizers-per-read inverts the filter. That ratio falls as abundance rises (r = −0.72): across the four samples its median is 84 for single-read species and 1.4 for species with 1,000 reads or more. A "ratio below 5" rule on SRR39106521 removed 80 % of the reads that had reached species rank, including the two species with the most reads, while keeping all 160 single-read species.

    Thresholding duplicity removes the most abundant species. Across all species rows duplicity hardly changes with abundance (r = +0.17), but the most deeply sequenced species have high values, because each of their minimizers is seen many times. Achromobacter denitrificans, the largest species in all four samples, has a duplicity of 23 to 74 on 42,000 to 1.1 million distinct minimizers. A duplicity cut-off of 10 prunes about as many species rows as the floor of 300 (65 % against 66 %), and its verdicts differ between samples less often (14 % of species against 20 %). But it would remove 73 to 93 % of each sample's table, A. denitrificans included, where the floor of 300 removes 1.3 to 5.8 %. With the gate's 1 % safety rail, which would keep A. denitrificans, it would still remove 16 to 65 %. Duplicity is therefore reported, not thresholded.

    Flagging outliers against a fitted trend line finds no distinct population. Fitting log(distinct minimizers) against log(reads) and taking residuals looks principled, but the residuals are essentially normal: in SRR39106521 a "2 SD below the line" rule flagged 14 of 578 species, where a normal distribution predicts 13.1. There is no separable cluster to find, so the rule just removes the bottom few percent of any sample. Worse, because the line is fitted per sample, the same species can fall on opposite sides of it in different samples of one study.

    The conclusion for the design: use one fixed floor for every sample of a run, so that every sample is judged against the same number, and treat the trend line as a ranking for the eye rather than a test.
