    Bacterial COI homologues (reference set)¶

    COI primers do not amplify only animals. Most bacteria that respire oxygen carry genes homologous to the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I, and common COI primer pairs amplify some of them, those of Wolbachia above all. MIDORI2, the COI reference of MetaFlux, contains only records filed under eukaryotes, so an amplicon of bacterial origin cannot be recognised as such: it can only be assigned to a eukaryote, usually an animal.

    MetaFlux therefore uses a second reference: a set of bacterial and archaeal subunit-I genes taken from the species representatives of the Genome Taxonomy Database, GTDB (Parks et al. 2026). For every ASV, MetaFlux compares its best identity to this set (B) with its best identity to MIDORI2 (E). An ASV with B > E is reported in a separate table of bacterial COI homologues. The same set is used once, when MIDORI2 is prepared, to remove MIDORI2 records that are themselves bacterial COI homologues filed under eukaryotic names.

    Names

    COI, or COX1, names the mitochondrial gene. The genes of this set are its bacterial and archaeal homologues: subunit I of the heme-copper oxidases (genes coxA or ctaD, cyoB, qoxB and ccoN, among others) and of the related nitric oxide reductases, which the build does not separate (step 3). This manual calls them bacterial COI homologues; "bacterial" includes the archaea. File names, settings and output columns keep the short form cox1 (for example bacterial_cox1_gtdb-r232.v1 and bacterial_cox1_asvs.tsv). The README of the Zenodo release and the messages of MetaFlux 2.6 use the earlier name, bacterial COX1.

    You do not build this set

    MetaFlux downloads the released set and checks its SHA-256 sum. This page explains how the set was built, why each step is done as it is, how other tools deal with the same problem, and what the set cannot do.

    All rules on this page were set on a development sample of 3,785 species representatives of GTDB R226, the release before R232. The sample contains all 485 species of the order Rickettsiales (56 of them Wolbachia), because this order holds Wolbachia, Rickettsia and other symbionts of arthropods, plus 3,000 other bacteria and 300 archaea drawn per phylum (at least three per phylum, the rest in proportion to the number of species in the phylum). Because Rickettsiales are over-represented, shares given for "all bacteria" are weighted back to the number of GTDB species in each group. The rules were fixed before the set was built from GTDB R232, except the evidence rule of step 6 and its use in check 5.4, which were revised during the build. The set was then tested once on data not used during development (step 7).

    Known issues in version 1

    Checks made after the release found that check 5.4 compared each gene only with the first 20 MIDORI2 records VSEARCH reached, and VSEARCH reaches long records first. With a full search, five genes of version 1 are 80.6–92.2 % identical to eukaryotic MIDORI2 records over at least 300 aligned bases, closer than to any gene of another genome, and should have been left out: GB_GCA_004211595.1|SEDO01000025.1_7 (filed under Pedobacter, 92.2 % identical to the chrysophyte record KF697361.1), GB_GCA_043769975.1|JAYVIS010000415.1_2, GB_GCA_002729235.1|PBTY01000104.1_1, GB_GCA_041191385.1|JBEEPA010000007.1_2 and GB_GCA_964599255.1|CAZRFT010000383.1_1. Three more genes are 82–83 % identical to the mitochondrial COX1 of the green alga Ostreococcus tauri and pass only because each is the closest gene of another genome for the others: GB_GCA_002726005.1|PBRS01000001.1_23, GB_GCA_963999645.1|CAXEUY010000032.1_4 and GB_GCA_002700775.1|PAEL01000127.1_2.

    On the MIDORI2 side, record KM679400.1 (Mycetophila lunata), counted as certainly mitochondrial, carries a Tisiphia gene in its first 600 bases; record KR036430.1 (Picromerus bidens) is a Wolbachia sequence; and 57 records that the build judged bacterial from their codons were left off the list. All of them stay in the MIDORI2 file that MetaFlux 2.6 prepares.

    Version 2 of the set, planned for MetaFlux 2.7.0, corrects these. Until then: an ASV in bacterial_cox1_asvs.tsv whose best_bacterial_gene is one of the eight genes above is probably a protist or a green alga, and an ASV in the main tables named Mycetophila lunata or Picromerus bidens may be bacterial (its B and E are in coi_screens.tsv).

    How MetaFlux uses the set¶

    For a COI run, MetaFlux downloads the release and MIDORI2, removes from MIDORI2 the records that step 6 marks as bacterial, and computes for every ASV its best identity to the cleaned MIDORI2 (E) and to the set (B). Both identities are computed as described in step 5 and must cover at least 80 % of the ASV.

    • B > E. The ASV is moved from the main ASV and taxonomy tables to bacterial_cox1_asvs.tsv, with its read counts, B, E and its best bacterial match. In the read-tracking table, the column post_bacterial_cox1 holds the reads left after the move; the moved reads are post_frame_check minus post_bacterial_cox1. Nothing is deleted, so that, for example, a Wolbachia infection stays visible.
    • All other ASVs. E and the best MIDORI2 match are reported for every ASV, and ASVs with E below 80 % are flagged.

    The 80 % flag marks ASVs that are far from everything in MIDORI2. In the development tests, at most 5 % of genuine animal sequences whose genus was missing from MIDORI2 fell below 80 %, and the flag alone marked 64–90 % of bacterial amplicons, depending on the primer pair. A flagged ASV may therefore be a eukaryote of a group that MIDORI2 covers poorly, or a bacterium that the set does not contain. Removing flagged ASVs would cost too many genuine eukaryotes (see "What MetaFlux does, and why"), so they are only flagged.

    The list of bacterial records belongs to MIDORI2 GB273. MetaFlux stops when the list names records that are missing from the MIDORI2 file it prepares, as a list made for another release would; a new MIDORI2 release needs a new list, made with step 6 of the build scripts. Settings are described on the COI marker page.

    Why a bacterial reference is needed¶

    Bacterial COI homologues in COI libraries¶

    Subunit I of the heme-copper oxygen reductases is the catalytic subunit of the terminal oxidases of aerobic respiration. The enzymes form three families, A, B and C. Family A contains the mitochondrial enzyme and the aa3-type oxidases of many bacteria, and is split into subtypes A1 and A2 (Pereira et al. 2001). Heme-copper oxidases are widely distributed in Bacteria and Archaea (Sousa et al. 2012), and many genomes carry several of them. The bacterial genes are known as ctaD or coxA (aa3-type cytochrome c oxidase), cyoB (bo3-type quinol oxidase), qoxB (aa3-600 quinol oxidase) and ccoN (cbb3-type oxidase, family C).

    COI is not a general marker for bacteria. Wolbachia is the exception: coxA is one of the five loci of its multilocus sequence typing scheme (Baldo et al. 2006), and Nowak et al. (2026) use insect COI barcode data to survey Wolbachia strains.

    COI primers bind the bacterial genes because the primer sites lie in conserved parts of the protein. Nowak et al. (2026, preprint) searched 59,318 bacterial RefSeq genomes: 36.8 % carry a gene annotated as aa3-like ctaD/coxA, and in these the reverse primer jgHCO2198 of the Leray primer pairs matches the gene perfectly in 79.8 % of genomes, BR2 in 70.6 %, but the forward primer BF3 in only 1.9 %. In the development sample, among the genomes that carry a family-A gene, the shares are almost the same: 78.0 %, 64.6 % and 1.4 % (weighted to GTDB).

    A single primer that binds is not enough: a product forms only when both primers of a pair bind. An in-silico PCR on the development sample therefore counted a genome as amplified by a primer pair when both primers bind its DNA, facing each other, and the stretch between them has the expected length (418, 313 and 205 bp for the three pairs, ± 30 bp). For the table, each primer could have at most one mismatch, so that it counts the products likely to form in a real PCR; the last two bases at its 3′ end had to match, and insertions or deletions were not allowed. The tests of B > E below use every product with up to three mismatches, so that they include more distant amplicons. The table gives the share of genomes amplified in each group.

    Share of genomes that give a PCR product with each primer pair:

    Primer pair Wolbachia (56 genomes) other Rickettsiales (429 genomes) all bacteria (weighted to GTDB)
    BF3/BR2 93 % 25 % 3.2 %
    Leray-XT 70 % 15 % 0.2 %
    fwhF2/fwhR2n 0 % 0.5 % 0 %

    For example, BF3/BR2 gives a product in 52 of the 56 Wolbachia genomes, but in only about 3 of every 100 bacterial species. The last column is an estimate for all bacterial species in GTDB: the sample holds every Rickettsiales species but only a few genomes of each other phylum, so each group is counted in proportion to its number of GTDB species. The reverse primers bind widely; the forward primer decides which bacteria amplify.

    How much of a library is bacterial depends on the sample:

    • Water eDNA. In samples from UK estuarine and coastal sites, 45.8 % of the Leray-XT reads had a bacterial best hit in NCBI nt, and so did 55 % of the reads of a fish-targeted, less degenerate COI primer pair (Collins et al. 2019).
    • Insects. The best-documented source is Wolbachia. Its COI appeared in 35.1 % of 7,901 BF3/BR2 libraries of single insects, at a median of 1.8 % of the reads where present (Nowak et al. 2026). Smith et al. (2012) found it in 0.16 % of 1.09 million Sanger barcode traces of insect specimens.

    With a reference made only of eukaryotic records, these sequences cannot be recognised:

    • No informative top rank. MIDORI2 has the same top rank (Eukaryota) in every record, so SINTAX (Edgar 2016) reports it with support 1.00 for any sequence, bacterial or not.
    • A Metazoa filter keeps them. When the MIDORI2 headers are given a kingdom rank, taken from MIDORI2's own taxonomy files, SINTAX assigned 42–69 % of the bacterial amplicons of the in-silico PCR to Metazoa with a confidence of at least 0.8, and 94–100 % of the Wolbachia amplicons.
    • Pseudogene filters pass them. These filters reject ASVs with a stop codon in frame under the invertebrate mitochondrial code, where only TAA and TAG are stops. A gene of a bacterium that uses the bacterial code (table 11), as almost all do, has no TAA or TAG inside its reading frame, so it passes.
    • MIDORI2 holds bacterial records itself. MIDORI2 is built from the eukaryotic divisions of GenBank (Leray et al. 2022), so a bacterial sequence enters it when it was submitted under a eukaryote's name, usually an animal's. With the genes of the development sample alone, 132 records of MIDORI2 release GB273 (built from GenBank release 273) were at least 90 % identical to a bacterial gene (step 6).

    How other tools handle it¶

    COI tools handle non-target sequences in two ways.

    Bacteria in the reference.

    • CO1 Classifier. The CO1 Classifier (Porter & Hajibabaei 2018), the COI reference used with MetaWorks (Porter & Hajibabaei 2022), states that "bacterial outgroup sequences were added"; how they were chosen is not documented.
      • Version 5.1.0 holds 2,216,547 records, 262 of them bacterial. 229 of these are Wolbachia COI barcode-region records from GenBank.
      • It is distributed for the RDP classifier (Wang et al. 2007), and since version 5.0.0 also in SINTAX and BLAST formats.
      • With pseudogene filtering switched on, the MetaWorks COI workflow keeps only ESVs (exact sequence variants, MetaWorks' term for ASVs) assigned to the target group (Arthropoda in the shipped configuration), so bacterial ESVs are dropped together with the other non-target ESVs.
    • COInr. COInr (Meglécz 2023) includes all cellular organisms, and its documentation states that the taxonomic origin of a sequence is taken at face value from the source database.
      • Its release of 3 May 2026 contains 9,775 bacterial sequences from 970 genera, 9,454 of them from BOLD.
      • VTAM and mkLTG point their users to ready-made files of the release of 6 May 2022, whose bacterial content was not counted for this page.
    • MJOLNIR. The COI references of MJOLNIR contain some prokaryotes, mostly Rickettsiales. These are db_COI_MBPK (Wangensteen et al. 2018), built by in-silico PCR on EMBL and from BOLD, and the later DUFA, built from BOLD, NCBI and local sequences. In its final step, MJOLNIR3 removes by default the MOTUs (molecular operational taxonomic units, clusters of similar sequences) assigned to prokaryotes; in the original MJOLNIR this step is off by default.
    • DARN. DARN (Zafeiropoulos et al. 2021) places COI sequences on a reference tree that includes 463 bacterial and 21 archaeal consensus sequences.
      • The bacterial ones were built from 3,917 bacterial COI sequences from BOLD, and from 9,154 bacterial sequences whose DNA was extracted from genomes for the proteins that Pfam assigns to COX1 (PF00115). 217 archaeal sequences were obtained in the same way.
      • In the authors' test with mock data, a BLAST search against the consensus sequences assigned all bacterial sequences to eukaryotes, while placement on the tree recognised all of them.
    • A study-specific MIDORI. Nowak et al. (2026) added alphaproteobacterial sequences to MIDORI release GB239 for their study.
    • BOLD. Up to version 3, BOLD checked uploaded sequences against a library of contaminants that includes Wolbachia and flagged matching records (Smith et al. 2012). No description of this check was found for BOLD version 5.

    A eukaryote-oriented reference with similarity limits or taxonomic filters.

    • VTAM. VTAM (González et al. 2023) assigns the lowest taxonomic group that contains at least 90 % of the BLAST hits, trying identity steps from 100 % down to 70 %. Its documentation notes that when this group "could only be defined at 80% or lower identity level, the results are not very robust".
    • eKOI. When re-analysing 15 published COI datasets against eKOI and MIDORI2, the eKOI authors (González-Miguéns et al. 2025) ignored ASVs below 84 % similarity to the references, a value they recommend applying with caution.
    • Anacapa. The BLCA classifier of the Anacapa Toolkit (Curd et al. 2019) considers hits of at least 80 % identity.
    • BOLDigger3 and apscale-blast. BOLDigger3 (version 3.0.3; Buchner & Leese 2020) and apscale-blast (version 2.0.6) limit the assigned rank by identity.
      • BOLDigger3: 97, 95, 90, 85 and 75 % from species to class, and 50 % for phylum.
      • apscale-blast: 98, 95, 90, 87 and 85 % from species to class.
    • nf-core/ampliseq. nf-core/ampliseq 3.0.0 (Straub et al. 2020) offers MIDORI2 CO1 for its DADA2 classifier and for a VSEARCH consensus classifier (90 % identity, full query coverage). It adds an optional codon filter and a name-based exclusion that by default removes mitochondria and chloroplasts.
    • BGE Bioscan. The BGE Bioscan workflow for BF3/BR2 classifies ASVs with SINTAX against BOLDistilled and keeps those assigned to Animalia, and to a class, with a confidence of at least 0.8.

    With the tools of the second group, what happens to a bacterial ASV depends on its identity to the nearest eukaryotic record. Below the lowest limit it stays unassigned; above that limit it is assigned to a eukaryotic group, at the rank that its identity allows; and where only the target group is kept, it is removed unless it was assigned to that group. No published measurement of how often each case occurs for bacterial ASVs was found, nor a study that compares the two approaches. The next section gives the measurements made for MetaFlux.

    What MetaFlux does, and why¶

    MetaFlux compares each ASV with both references. The choice rests on three results of the development tests, which used the following design.

    • Bacterial queries were the in-silico PCR products of the development sample, split into two halves by species (and, in a second run, by genus). Each half served once as queries and once as the bacterial reference for the other half, so that no query could find its own species (or genus). MIDORI2 was cleaned with the reference half only.
    • Eukaryotic queries were genuine COI sequences from MIDORI2, compared after every record of their own species, genus, family or order had been left out of MIDORI2, to mimic organisms that the reference lacks.

    The bacterial reference in these tests was made of in-silico PCR products, not of whole genes. The final test (step 7) repeats the measurements with the R232 gene set.

    First, no identity limit separates bacteria from eukaryotes. Bacterial amplicons and eukaryotic sequences whose relatives are missing from the reference fall in the same range of identities to MIDORI2:

    Primer pair Identity limit needed to catch 95 % of bacterial amplicons Genuine animal sequences lost at that limit (family missing from MIDORI2)
    BF3/BR2 83 % 36 %
    Leray-XT 89 % 72 %
    fwhF2/fwhR2n 95 % 87 %

    Second, the comparison B > E recognised 94 % of the bacterial amplicons, pooled over the three primer pairs: BF3/BR2 96 %, Leray-XT 91 %, fwhF2/fwhR2n 85 %, and Wolbachia 97 %. Of the genuine animal sequences, it moved none to the bacterial side when their species or genus was missing from MIDORI2, 0.04 % when their family was missing and 0.14 % when their order was missing.

    Third, simpler variants are weaker or add nothing.

    • A one-sided screen that removes any ASV at least 78 % identical to a bacterial amplicon recognised 71 % of the bacterial amplicons. The limit of 78 % lies just above the highest identity of any genuine eukaryotic test sequence to the bacterial amplicons (77.6 %, once two genomes with eukaryotic contamination were left out of the bacterial reference), so it is the lowest limit that removed no eukaryote.
    • Bacterial sequences added to the SINTAX reference pulled none of 4,560 eukaryotic test sequences into Bacteria, but added at most 0.6 percentage points to the share recognised by B > E. MetaFlux does not add them.

    The build, step by step¶

    Step 0. Source of the bacterial sequences¶

    Three sources were considered.

    1. Bacterial records labelled COI in GenBank or BOLD, as used by COInr and the CO1 Classifier.
      • They are ready to use, but their labels come from the submitters, and a few are wrong: two BOLD records labelled Alphaproteobacteria are COI of flies (94–97 % identical to fly records, GC content 31–37 %).
      • Most records also come from a few lineages. Wolbachia makes up 229 of the 262 bacterial records of the CO1 Classifier, and Actinomycetes and Alphaproteobacteria three quarters of those in COInr.
    2. In-silico PCR products cut from genomes. They are close to what the primers amplify, but tied to the primer pairs used for the build; any other primer pair would need a new build.
    3. Whole genes predicted in genomes. Every subunit-I gene of every genome, independent of primers, with the taxonomy of the genome. DARN used this source for part of its bacterial sequences.

    MetaFlux uses the third source, taken from GTDB, for three reasons:

    • all GTDB genomes are classified in a single taxonomy;
    • each species has a representative genome;
    • GTDB distributes its own gene predictions for every representative.

    The build uses these predictions as they are and does not predict genes again, so every gene in the set can be traced to GTDB's annotation of the release.

    Only species representatives are used. Recognition does not need the query's own species: in the development tests, with the species left out of the bacterial reference, 94 % of the bacterial amplicons were still recognised, and 78 % with the genus left out. One genome per species is therefore enough, and the other 700,000 genomes of the release would multiply the size of the set with little gain.

    The cost of taking genes from genomes is that many representatives are metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). MAGs often cut genes at contig ends, and they can contain contigs of other organisms, eukaryotes included. Step 4 deals with cut genes, step 5 with contamination.

    Step 1. Integrity and translation¶

    The gene file and the metadata are checked against GTDB's published MD5 sums. For each genome, the number of genes in the file must equal the protein count in GTDB's metadata; a genome that fails is left out and listed.

    The build reads the DNA genes and translates them itself, rather than also downloading GTDB's protein file (another 123 GB). Protein and DNA then always come from the same record. The price is that the genetic code has to be worked out for each genome:

    • The default. Each gene is translated with the bacterial genetic code (NCBI table 11).
    • Table 4. For some lineages, mainly Mycoplasmatales (GTDB phylum Bacillota_I; NCBI phylum Mycoplasmatota), GTDB predicts genes with table 4, in which TGA codes for tryptophan.
    • The table is not in the files. The table GTDB used is not stored in the release files. The metadata give only NCBI's table, and for 92,894 of the 189,801 bacterial species representatives they give none.
    • The rule. The table is therefore read back from the genes. Under table 11 TGA is a stop codon, so no gene predicted with table 11 can contain TGA inside its reading frame; under table 4, genes that encode tryptophan with TGA do. If more than 1 % of a genome's genes with both a start and a stop codon (partial=00) contain an in-frame TGA before their stop codon, the genome is translated with table 4.
    • Recording. The inferred table is recorded for every gene and compared with NCBI's table where NCBI gives one; disagreements are listed in the README.

    Two simpler rules were tested on the development sample and not used:

    • A rule by lineage ("Mollicutes use table 4") would have been wrong for 11 genomes: some Acholeplasmatales use table 11, and some table-4 genomes lie outside the Mollicutes.
    • The coding-density rule of GTDB-Tk disagreed with the table GTDB had used for 2 of the 3,785 genomes.

    The lineages concerned rarely carry respiratory oxidases. In the development sample, none of the 15 genomes predicted with table 4 (10 Mycoplasmatales, 3 BD1-5, Anadelfobacter and Stammera) carried a candidate gene. The README reports how many R232 genomes the 1 % rule assigned to table 4.

    Step 2. Finding subunit-I genes¶

    All proteins are searched with HMMER hmmsearch and the Pfam model PF00115 ("Cytochrome C and Quinol oxidase polypeptide I", Pfam release 38.2; Paysan-Lafosse et al. 2025). The search uses the model's gathering threshold, the score above which Pfam's curators count a protein as a member of the family (34.8 bits for PF00115). PF00115 covers subunit I of all three oxidase families and of the related nitric oxide reductases.

    The Pfam models for COX2 (PF00116), COX3 (PF00510) and cytochrome b (PF00033) are searched at the same time. Their hits are used only to describe the contig on which a gene lies (step 5).

    Two alternatives were tested on the development sample and not used for selection:

    • A sequence motif of family A (W.[FW]GHPEV[YF]....P). It finds only subtype A1, and missed the 476 A2-type genes of the sample, among them every cyanobacterial aa3 oxidase.
    • The TIGRFAM models for the individual oxidase types. Their trusted cutoffs are the scores above which the curators consider every hit a true member of that oxidase type. They are set high so that proteins can be named safely, and are too strict for selection: they left 758 whole family-A genes without a label, and these held 143 of the 1,239 in-silico PCR products that lay in kept genes. The TIGRFAM models are therefore used only for labels (step 3).

    Gene annotations in RefSeq were not used either. They depend on the annotation pipeline: NCBI attaches the name "cbb3-type cytochrome c oxidase subunit I" to its copy of the generic PF00115 model, so genes recognised only by that model carry this name, family-B genes included.

    Step 3. Oxidase or not, and which family¶

    A protein can match PF00115 above the gathering threshold without being an oxidase subunit I. In the development sample, 249 of the 4,304 genes that passed all other checks (5.8 %) failed the oxidase rule below. 193 of them had two or more of the six ligand histidines replaced, most often the CuB pair His290 and His291, and 56 scored below 100 bits. Most were 425–500 amino acids long, with a median score of 55 bits. Such genes are kept out of the set and listed in the metadata table. None of the in-silico PCR products of the development sample lay in them.

    Each hit is aligned to PF00115 together with human COX1 (GenBank HQ713447.1). Human COX1 is included only to give the alignment columns their usual residue numbers; at the positions below, human and bovine numbering are identical.

    Bovine position Role Used for
    His61, His378 ligands of heme a oxidase
    His376 ligand of heme a3 oxidase
    His240, His290, His291 ligands of CuB oxidase
    Tyr244 cross-linked to His240 (helix VI) family A
    Glu242 proton channel, motif XGHPEV subtype A1
    Tyr238, Ser239 motif YSHPXV, replacing the A1 glutamate subtype A2
    • Oxidase. A hit counts as an oxidase subunit I if it scores at least 100 bits against PF00115 and at most one of the six histidines is replaced. One replacement is allowed, and a gap in the alignment is not counted as a replacement, because a single alignment to the Pfam model can misplace a residue in a divergent sequence.
    • Family A. A gene is assigned to family A if it carries Tyr244 together with Glu242 (subtype A1) or with Tyr238 and Ser239 (subtype A2); these are the residues that define the subtypes (Pereira et al. 2001).
    • Type label. The label (aa3 or caa3, bo3, aa3-600, cbb3) comes from the best model above its trusted cutoff among TIGR02891, TIGR02843, TIGR02882 and TIGR00780 (TIGRFAMs) and NF050116.1 (NCBIfam, aa3 oxidases of Actinomycetota), all from NCBI release hmm_PGAP 20.0 (Li et al. 2021).

    Family B has no HMM in Pfam or TIGRFAM, and family C carries its cross-linked tyrosine on helix VII instead of helix VI (Sousa et al. 2011). Genes of both families therefore remain "family not assigned" unless a TIGRFAM model names them. Nitric oxide reductases share the six histidine positions but lack the cross-linked tyrosine (Sousa et al. 2011), and under these rules they also end up as "family not assigned". They do no harm on the bacterial side of the comparison: a eukaryotic COI amplicon is far less similar to them than to MIDORI2, so they cannot raise B above E.

    All oxidase subunit-I genes are kept, not only family A:

    • Why. In the development sample, family A held every in-silico PCR product of the three primer pairs. Other primer pairs, or bacteria absent from the sample, may amplify genes of families B or C, and a set limited to family A would miss them.
    • The cost. A set of family A only would be about 40 % as large.
    • No risk. The other genes cannot cause a false B > E, for the reason given above.

    Step 4. Whole genes and fragments¶

    In MAGs, genes are often cut at contig ends. A gene counts as whole if it meets three conditions:

    • it has a start and a stop codon (partial=00 in GTDB's prediction);
    • it covers at least 70 % of the PF00115 model, counted over all its domain hits together, so that a protein whose alignment to the model is broken in two, for example by an insertion, is measured as one;
    • it is 400–1,100 amino acids long. Whole genes in the development sample had a median length of 555 amino acids. The range includes the fusions of subunits I and III found in some haloarchaea, Thermus and Deinococcus (517–901 amino acids; 43 genes in the development sample) and two haloarchaeal genes of 1,007 and 1,065 amino acids. No candidate was longer; the upper limit is there to catch gene predictions that run on into a neighbouring gene.

    A gene that is not whole is kept as a fragment if it has at least 200 amino acids.

    • Why fragments are kept. The primers amplify only 200–420 bp of a gene of about 1,600 bp, so a fragment that covers that region works as well as a whole gene in the comparison. In the development sample, 5.9 % of the in-silico PCR products lay only in fragments, mostly from MAGs of uncultured lineages.
    • Why they are kept apart. A fragment shows only part of the residues of step 3, so the evidence that it is an oxidase is weaker, and a gene cut in two would be counted twice in copy numbers.
    • Why 200 amino acids. Shorter pieces carry too few residues and too little sequence for the checks of step 5; the shortest fragment that held an in-silico PCR product had 253 amino acids.

    Fragments go into a separate file. MetaFlux uses both files to compute B.

    Whole genes and fragments together held 1,239 of the 1,259 in-silico PCR products of the development sample (98.4 %). Of the other 20, five came from contigs of eukaryotic origin and were rightly left out, five lay across a gene split in two and were partly kept, and ten lay at loci where the gene is broken by a frameshift or an internal stop codon.

    Step 5. Contamination checks¶

    GTDB admits a genome only if CheckM (Parks et al. 2015), which uses marker genes, and CheckM2 (Chklovski et al. 2023), a machine-learning model, both estimate completeness of at least 50 %, contamination below 5 % and a quality score of at least 50 (Parks et al. 2026). Among the genomes that pass, it prefers type material and high-quality assemblies as species representatives. A single foreign contig carrying one gene is below what such genome-wide estimates can detect, so every candidate gene is tested individually.

    Two other approaches are possible:

    • Leaving out MAGs would remove most contamination. But for many uncultured lineages a MAG is the only genome, and those lineages hold most of the in-silico PCR products found only in fragments (step 4).
    • Classifying each contig taxonomically would need the genome sequences, another archive of about 180 GB that the build does not download.

    Without the genome sequences, the GC content and length of a contig are estimated from the genes on it. In the development sample this gave the same GC verdict as the contig sequence for all 5,060 candidate genes, and the same contig-length flag for all but one.

    Four checks remove a gene.

    • 5.1 Reading frame. The DNA length is a multiple of 3, and there is no internal stop codon under the table chosen in step 1.
    • 5.2 GC content. The GC content of the gene, and that of its contig, lies within 0.15 of the genome's GC content.
      • The largest difference observed for a genuine gene in the development sample was 0.132.
      • Example: a thraustochytrid-like COX1 in a planctomycete MAG (gene GC 0.39, genome GC 0.66) fails this check. Check 5.3 cannot catch it, because thraustochytrids read TGA as a stop, so none of their records count as certainly mitochondrial; check 5.4 removes it as well (80.1 % identical to Aurantiochytrium).
    • 5.3 Match to mitochondrial COI. The gene is less than 90 % identical, over at least 80 % of the shorter sequence, to every MIDORI2 record that is certainly mitochondrial.
      • Why this works. Animal and fungal mitochondria read TGA as tryptophan, and most of them use it often, while a gene of a bacterium that uses table 11 cannot contain TGA in frame. Lineages that use TGA rarely, such as mushrooms and liver flukes (step 6), have few certainly mitochondrial records, so a COI of theirs binned into a MAG is left to step 6 and check 5.4.
      • Why 90 %, over the shorter sequence. Genuine bacterial genes of the development sample reached at most 85.4 % (a Tisiphia gene, against a record that is probably chimeric), while the copepod COI found in a bacterial MAG (see "Contamination removed before any check" below) is 99.5 % identical to a mitochondrial record. Coverage is measured on the shorter sequence because many MIDORI2 records are barcodes of about 650 bp, which cannot cover 80 % of a whole gene.
      • The definition. A record counts as certainly mitochondrial if none of its three reading frames is open under the bacterial code (each has at least two TAA, TAG or TGA), its reading frame contains at least two TGA codons, and TGA is used at least as often as TGG.
      • Why it is strict. MIDORI2 contains bacterial records. A looser definition admitted two bacterial records filed as a fish and a planthopper, which then caused 19 genuine bacterial genes to be rejected.
      • How many records qualify. MIDORI2 GB273 is used in its UNIQ NUC_SP variant: every unique sequence of each species, with records whose species name is incomplete, such as "sp." or "cf.", included. Of its 3,213,682 records, 2,904,469 meet the definition.
    • 5.4 Closer to eukaryotes than to bacteria. The gene's best identity to MIDORI2 is at least 80 % and higher than its best identity to the genes of the other GTDB genomes. This check is run after step 6, on MIDORI2 without the records removed there, so that a bacterial record filed under an animal's name cannot make a genuine bacterial gene look eukaryotic.
      • Records whose codons look bacterial do not count. For the same reason, a MIDORI2 record whose own TGA/TGG counts look bacterial by the codon test of step 6 is not used as evidence here. Most such records are less than 90 % identical to every gene, so step 6 never examined them, but they are close enough to the genes of their own bacteria to make those genes look eukaryotic. In R232, 58 records were left out of E this way, and 20 genes that they would have made check 5.4 reject passed.
      • Outcome. Such a gene is removed if it is a fragment or if its GC content differs from the genome's by more than 0.08. Otherwise it is marked "review" and also left out of the release, so that no manual decision enters the build.
      • In the development sample this check failed three candidates and marked 20 whole genes for review:
        • the thraustochytrid gene of check 5.2;
        • a fungal mitochondrial piece in a Rickettsia MAG (89.3 % identical to Phialocephala, just below the limit of check 5.3). At 55 amino acids it also falls below the floor of step 4;
        • a 274-amino-acid Polaromonas fragment, probably removed wrongly. Its best MIDORI2 match, MF319222.1, is filed as the clam Corbicula fluminea but has a GC content of 0.64 and one TGA in frame, and is probably a bacterial record itself.

    How identity is computed. Identities in checks 5.3 and 5.4, in step 6 and in MetaFlux itself are computed from the VSEARCH alignment:

    • identical A/C/G/T columns are divided by all aligned columns;
    • end gaps are left out, and internal gaps count as differences;
    • columns where either sequence has N or another ambiguity code are left out of both counts.

    VSEARCH's own identity counts a column as identical whenever the two symbols can stand for the same base, so an N matches anything. Its option --n_mismatch changes this for N but not for the other ambiguity codes. About 9 % of MIDORI2 records contain ambiguity codes, some in long runs, and a sequence aligned over such a run would otherwise appear identical. In the development tests, VSEARCH's own value was inflated in this way for 8–15 % of the bacterial amplicons; one archaeal amplicon appeared 100 % identical to an insect record, while its best identity over A/C/G/T columns was 52.8 %.

    Three observations are recorded as flags but do not remove a gene:

    • Cross-phylum cluster. The gene clusters at 95 % identity with genes from another phylum, as the same contaminant binned into MAGs of different lineages would. There were none in the development sample. In R232, 524 kept genes carry the flag, in 182 clusters, 154 of them made only of MAGs or single-cell genomes; most join a Pseudomonadota gene to one of another phylum. Such a gene is still bacterial, so B is not affected, but the bacterial lineage MetaFlux reports for an ASV that matches it can be the wrong one.
    • Mitochondrion-like contig. The gene lies on a contig shorter than 20 kb that also carries cytochrome b and COX2 or COX3, as a mitochondrial contig would. Bacterial operons in which the bc1 complex lies next to the oxidase genes trigger this flag too (37 genes in the development sample, all bacterial; 2,247 in R232), which is why it is not a filter. The one contig of eukaryotic origin in the development sample, which carries the thraustochytrid-like gene of check 5.2 together with COX2, COX3 and cytochrome b, is 29.5 kb long and is not flagged.
    • Duplicate copy. Two whole genes of the same genome are at least 99 % identical, as happens when a contig is duplicated in an assembly.

    Contamination removed before any check. An animal or fungal mitochondrial COI binned into a bacterial MAG contains TGA codons, which the gene prediction reads as stops under table 11. At most, short open reading frames are predicted at such a locus, and they fall below the 200-amino-acid floor of step 4. In the development sample, no gene was predicted at a copepod COI in a bacterial MAG, and a fungal COX1 in a Rickettsia MAG gave only a 55-amino-acid open reading frame.

    Step 6. Cleaning MIDORI2¶

    The genes that pass checks 5.1–5.3 are compared with every record of MIDORI2 GB273 (UNIQ NUC_SP). The NUC_SP variant contains every record of the NUC variant, so the resulting list applies to both.

    A close match means that one side is misfiled. Genuine COI and its bacterial homologues are never this close: in the development tests, no genuine eukaryotic sequence was more than 77.6 % identical to a bacterial gene. A record at least 90 % identical to a gene (identity as in step 5, over at least 80 % of the shorter sequence) is therefore either a bacterial sequence filed under a eukaryote's name, or the gene is a eukaryotic sequence binned into a bacterial genome. Two independent lines of evidence say which.

    • The gene's side: are its relatives bacterial or mitochondrial? A genuine bacterial gene usually has relatives in other GTDB genomes; a mitochondrial COI in a MAG has mitochondrial relatives instead.
      • The gene looks bacterial when its closest gene in another genome is at least 80 % identical and at least as close as its closest certainly mitochondrial record (step 5.3).
      • It looks mitochondrial when a certainly mitochondrial record is at least 80 % identical and closer than any gene of another genome.
      • Otherwise it gives no evidence. Only certainly mitochondrial records are used on this side, because they cannot be bacterial: they carry TGA codons in frame.
    • The record's side: do its tryptophan codons fit its lineage? In a bacterial gene every tryptophan is TGG, because TGA is a stop. Most animal and fungal mitochondria read TGA as tryptophan, but how often they use it instead of TGG differs widely between lineages. In the insect records of MIDORI2, 94 % of tryptophan codons are TGA; in the mushrooms (Agaricomycetes), 21 %; in the liver-fluke family Fasciolidae, 15 %. A record without TGA is therefore strong evidence in an insect and none in a mushroom.
      • The record's TGA and TGG counts are compared under two explanations. As a bacterial gene, a TGA can only be an error, taken to occur at 1 in 100 tryptophan codons. As a genuine COI of its lineage, each tryptophan is TGA with the share seen in the lineage's other MIDORI2 records: those of its family, or of the lowest higher rank (order, class, phylum) whose records hold at least 200 tryptophan codons. Records in conflict with a gene are left out of these shares.
      • The ratio of the two likelihoods counts as evidence when it is at least 20 in either direction, the level that Kass and Raftery (1995) call strong.
      • In lineages whose mitochondria read TGA as a stop (plants, brown algae, oomycetes, diatoms, chrysophytes, and green algae such as Micromonas), the codons give no evidence.

    The decision. A record is removed when at least one line of evidence says bacterial and none says mitochondrial. Otherwise it is listed and kept, and check 5.4 then judges the gene with the record still in MIDORI2. Examples from the R232 build:

    • Kept: six mushroom records. Six records of Rhizoctonia, Ganoderma and Fomes were 91–98 % identical to a gene in a MAG of a soil archaeon (Nitrososphaeraceae). Their lineages use TGA for only 1–5 % of tryptophan codons, so their TGA counts are no evidence. The gene has no relative in any other genome. The COX1 model matches only amino acids 12–151 of its 531, and the rest carries the two LAGLIDADG motifs of the homing endonucleases encoded in group I introns (Pfam PF00961): the gene is the first exon of a fungal COX1 read on into the open reading frame of its intron, a common arrangement in fungal mitochondria. The closest record, KC352446.1 from a Rhizoctonia solani mitochondrial genome, is that intron reading frame alone, filed in MIDORI2 as COI. The records stay, and check 5.4 removes the gene.
    • Removed: a beetle record with one TGA. A record filed as the carrion beetle Calosilpha brunnicollis has one TGA among seven tryptophan codons and is 96.8 % identical to a gene of Aquirickettsiella, a bacterial pathogen of arthropods. Its family uses TGA for 93 % of tryptophan codons, so one TGA in seven is about 60,000 times likelier for a bacterial gene with one sequencing error than for the beetle's own COI.
    • Removed: three chrysophyte records. Three records of Dinobryon pediforme are 99.3–99.6 % identical to a gene of a MAG of the Paracaedibacterales, an order of alphaproteobacteria that includes endosymbionts of protists. Chrysophytes read TGA as a stop, so the codons say nothing, but the gene has relatives in four other genomes of the order (75–86 %).
    • Removed: a liver-fluke record. A record filed as Fasciola hepatica is 96.0 % identical to the gene of Neorickettsia findlayensis, an endosymbiont of trematodes whose complete genome has relatives in two other Neorickettsia genomes (89–90 %). Liver flukes rarely use TGA, so the codons say nothing either way, and the gene decides.

    In R232, 186 records were at least 90 % identical to one of 95 genes that passed check 5.3, and 170 of them were removed:

    • By lineage. 137 of the removed records are filed under arthropods, 13 under diatoms, 6 under chordates, and 14 under seven other groups (gastrotrichs, chrysophytes, red algae, annelids, molluscs, a dinoflagellate and a liver fluke). The 16 listed records are the six mushroom records, four Micromonas records, four nematode records (Plectus, whose gene looks mitochondrial), and one record each of a synurophyte and a gastrotrich.
    • By evidence. 128 records were removed on both lines of evidence, 40 on the gene's alone and 2 on the codons alone. Of the 40, 13 belong to lineages whose mitochondria read TGA as a stop; the other 27 have too few tryptophan codons, or belong to lineages that use TGA too rarely, for the codons to tell. 12 records were listed for lack of evidence, 4 because their gene looks mitochondrial.
    • Genes. Of the 95 genes in conflict with a record, 89 look bacterial, 1 mitochondrial, and 5 give no evidence (no relative of at least 80 % on either side).

    Why two lines of evidence. The first version of this step, fixed before the R232 build, removed a record when it had no TGA in frame and its lineage reads TGA as tryptophan, and kept every record with at least one TGA. Applied to R232, it went wrong in two ways:

    • it removed five of the six mushroom records above, those without a TGA, because a missing TGA is no evidence in lineages that rarely use TGA, and the gene they matched was never examined;
    • it kept bacterial records with a single TGA, such as the beetle record above. Left in MIDORI2, such records made check 5.4 reject the genes of the bacteria they come from: a single record filed as the clam Corbicula fluminea (MF319222.1: one TGA among six tryptophan codons, 97.9 % identical to a Paenacidovorax gene) made 22 genes of Burkholderiales fail.

    Under the first rule, check 5.4 left out 102 genes. With the revised rule, and with the records whose codons look bacterial left out of E (check 5.4), it leaves out 24; the other 78 pass, and no gene fails that passed before. Of the 24, 16 are close to the COI of green algae, cryptophytes, diatoms, oomycetes, a malawimonad, a fungus or a nematode and are most likely eukaryotic contaminants; the other 8 are close to records filed as animals (a scallop, a gastropod, an amphipod, a shipworm) whose codons give no clear evidence, and are probably genuine bacterial genes left out.

    The rule was therefore revised before the final test. Because it was set while looking at the R232 records, it has not been tested on independent data. The README reports how many decisions change when each of its settings moves: none for an error rate of 0.003 or 0.03, a likelihood-ratio threshold of 10 or 50, or 100 or 500 tryptophan codons per lineage; 2 and 1 of the 186 when the 80 % of the gene's side moves to 78 or 82 %. Examining records from 88 % instead of 90 % would remove 6 of the 14 records between the two values; from 92 %, 15 records would be decided differently or no longer examined.

    MIDORI2 already excludes 2,215 GenBank records that an earlier study had found mislabelled at the order, class or phylum level (Leray et al. 2019, 2022). Bacterial sequences submitted under the host's name are a separate problem, which this step addresses.

    Step 7. Final test¶

    The rules were fixed before the R232 build, except the evidence rule of step 6 and its use in check 5.4, which were revised during the build; the final test came after that revision. It is run once, before release, on genomes and MIDORI2 records not used to set any rule, and it asks how the set handles bacteria and eukaryotes that the references do not contain.

    Bacterial amplicons¶

    • Test genomes. 3,476 R232 species representatives that are not in the development sample, chosen with the development design and a new random seed: all 176 such Rickettsiales (4 of them Wolbachia), 3,000 other bacteria drawn per phylum, and 300 archaea. All the Rickettsiales are taken, as in development, because the order holds Wolbachia, Rickettsia and other symbionts of arthropods.
    • In-silico PCR. The rules of development: both primers bind, facing each other, the insert between them is within 30 bp of the expected length, a primer may have up to three mismatches but none in its last two bases at the 3′ end, and insertions and deletions are not allowed. Three mismatches is permissive, on purpose: the test gets more amplicons, and more distant ones. The products with an intact reading frame under the bacterial code are the queries. The PCR is run on the genes that GTDB predicted in each genome, not on the genome sequence, which the build does not download: an amplicon lies inside the subunit-I gene, and in development this missed only the products in genes broken by a frameshift or an internal stop (10 of 1,259, 0.8 %).
    • Why most genomes give no product. 738 of the 3,476 genomes gave a product, 936 products in all: 803 of BF3/BR2, 102 of Leray-XT and 31 of fwhF2/fwhR2n. A genome gives one product per primer pair, or more when it carries several subunit-I genes.
      • Almost all products come from family-A genes, the family of the mitochondrial enzyme (928 of the 936 from subtype A1), and 1,648 of the test genomes (47 %) have no family-A gene in the set.
      • Where there is one, the primers decide. BF3/BR2 gives a product in a fifth of the other bacteria (610 of 3,000), but in only 86 (2.9 %) with at most one mismatch per primer, close to the 3.2 % of the development table. Leray-XT and fwhF2/fwhR2n give one in hardly any bacteria outside the Rickettsiales.
      • With at most one mismatch per primer, the products likely to form in a real PCR, 181 of the 936 remain.

    • Each amplicon is tested with its own species left out of the set. Every test genome is a GTDB species representative, so its own genes are in the set: its amplicon would find itself at 100 % identity and pass without telling anything. A bacterium in a real library is rarely the genome that GTDB holds; often its species is missing, sometimes its genus. B is therefore computed without the genes of the amplicon's own species, and, in a second run, without those of its whole genus, as for a bacterium of a genus absent from GTDB. The search keeps up to 3,000 hits per amplicon, and the hits to the genes left out are skipped.
    • The same on the MIDORI2 side. Step 6 removes from MIDORI2 the bacterial records filed under eukaryote names, which it finds through the genes that pass checks 5.1–5.3. A removed record whose closest gene belongs to the amplicon's own species (or genus) is put back for that amplicon, as if that species were missing from GTDB. This is the cautious choice: through the genes of other species, step 6 might still have removed the record. E is computed against MIDORI2 with these records back.
    • Measured is the share of amplicons with B > E: those that MetaFlux would recognise as bacterial.

    Eukaryotic sequences¶

    • Test records. 3,336 MIDORI2 GB273 records not used during development: 1,000 at the species level, 1,000 at the genus level, 858 at the family level and 478 at the order level, up to 250 taxa per group (arthropods, chordates, other animals, other eukaryotes). At the order level, and for the non-animal families, development had already used every eligible taxon, so these taxa are tested again with records not used before.
    • Each insert is tested with its own taxon left out of MIDORI2. Each record is cut to the inserts of the three primer pairs (10,008 inserts). For each insert, every record of its own species (genus, family or order) is left out of MIDORI2, as for an animal that MIDORI2 does not contain, and B is computed against the whole set.
    • Measured is the share with B > E: genuine eukaryotic sequences that MetaFlux would wrongly move to the table of bacterial COI homologues.

    Known contaminants¶

    As a check that nothing was lost, the contaminants found during development must still be rejected. Unlike the other tests, this one uses development data.

    Copy number¶

    How many subunit-I genes a genome carries depends on what is counted. In near-complete genomes of the development sample (CheckM2 completeness ≥ 90 %, contamination ≤ 5 %; shares weighted to GTDB), with the counts of the 120,230 near-complete bacterial genomes of R232 in brackets:

    • Family A is mostly single-copy. 65 % of the genomes that carry it have one copy and 35 % have two to five [R232: 64 % of 71,669 carriers have one copy, up to nine]. All 51 near-complete Wolbachia genomes that carry it have one copy, and so do 99 % of the other Rickettsiales [R232: 55 of 56 Wolbachia genomes have one copy, one has none].
    • All subunit-I genes together are mostly multi-copy. 69 % of carriers have two or more, up to nine, because many bacteria carry several oxidase types [R232: 67 % of 79,030 carriers, up to twelve]. In 90 % of the multi-copy genomes all copies are less than 80 % identical to each other; they are distinct genes, not assembly artefacts.

    All copies that pass the checks are kept. The primers can bind any of them, and MetaFlux uses only the best match of each ASV, so extra copies give a bacterial ASV more chances to find a close relative without biasing the result.

    Limitations, open issues and possible improvements¶

    Coverage of bacterial diversity. The set contains what GTDB contains. In the final test, 96.0 % of the amplicons were recognised with their genus left out of the set, against 98.4 % with only their species left out (78 % and 94 % in the development tests). Most of them were BF3/BR2 amplicons (803 of 936, 98.8 % recognised with the genus left out); for Leray-XT the share was 80.4 %, for fwhF2/fwhR2n 77.4 %. None of the 11 Wolbachia amplicons was recognised with its genus left out, because MIDORI2 holds Wolbachia coxA under animal names: a Wolbachia lineage missing from the set is recognised only if MIDORI2 holds no record of it under an animal's name (step 7). Lineages without cultured or assembled members are not represented.

    One genome per species. Variation within a species is represented by one genome. The development tests removed the whole species and still recognised 94 % of the amplicons, so an amplicon from a divergent strain should be recognised at least as often; this was not tested directly.

    Rules set on development data. These values were chosen after inspection of the development sample:

    • the residue rule of step 3;
    • the 200-amino-acid floor for fragments;
    • check 5.4, with its GC limit of 0.08;
    • the strict definition of certainly mitochondrial records.

    They are frozen for version 1 and tested once on data not used to set them. Other values are defensible; the README reports how many genes change verdict when twelve of the thresholds move, the fragment floor and the GC limit of check 5.4 among them (the residue rule and the definition of certainly mitochondrial records are not single values and are not varied). The evidence rule of step 6 is the exception: it was revised during the R232 build, after the first rule had been applied to the R232 records, so no independent data have tested it (step 6).

    Contaminants that can pass. The mitochondrial-match check (5.3) relies on TGA being read as tryptophan, so it cannot recognise contamination from plants, brown algae, oomycetes, diatoms or thraustochytrids. For these, only the GC check (5.2) and the closer-to-eukaryotes check (5.4) apply. A contaminant with a GC content close to that of the genome and no close relative in MIDORI2 would pass. Check 5.4 also misses a contaminant that occurs in more than one MAG, for example in MAGs from the same study, because each copy then finds a close match in another genome.

    Bacterial records left in MIDORI2. Step 6 removes a record only when it is at least 90 % identical to a gene that passes checks 5.1–5.3 and the evidence points to bacteria. Bacterial records of lineages that GTDB lacks, or just below 90 %, stay in MIDORI2 and in the E of every ASV. Such a record can also make check 5.4 reject a genuine bacterial gene, as happened to one Polaromonas fragment in development and probably to 8 genes in R232 (step 6).

    Genes left out for review. Whole genes marked "review" by check 5.4 are left out of version 1, although some may be genuine. Their lineages then have fewer genes on the bacterial side of the comparison.

    Eukaryotes moved to the bacterial table. In development, the comparison moved 0.04 % of animal sequences whose family was missing from MIDORI2, and 0.14 % of those whose order was missing. In groups that MIDORI2 covers poorly, a few genuine eukaryotic ASVs can therefore end up in the table of bacterial COI homologues; check that table before concluding that an expected group is absent.

    Labels of families B and C and of nitric oxide reductases. The family labels rest on one alignment to the Pfam model and on residues read from it. Divergent sequences can be misaligned, and nitric oxide reductases are not separated from families B and C. A dedicated classifier for the oxidase families was published (Sousa et al. 2011), but it is no longer available at the address given in the paper.

    Genetic codes. The table GTDB used is inferred from the genes, not read from the files. Lineages with genetic code 25 (Gracilibacteria, and Absconditabacteria, formerly SR1; both read TGA as glycine) are predicted by GTDB under table 4 or 11. Under table 11 some of their genes are truncated or missing; under table 4 their TGA codons are translated as tryptophan instead of glycine.

    The MIDORI2 list is a lower bound. Short records with few tryptophan codons, chimeric records (part animal, part bacterial), and records whose gene has no relatives in other genomes and whose codons say nothing are hard to judge; they are listed, not removed. MIDORI2 also holds sequences that are mitochondrial but not COI, such as the intron reading frame of step 6; they are left in place.

    Three primer pairs tested. The recognition rates were measured for BF3/BR2, Leray-XT and fwhF2/fwhR2n. The set itself does not depend on primers, but the rates for other primer pairs are not known.

    Shares count taxa, not reads. All test results are shares of genomes or sequences, not of reads. In a real library a few abundant ASVs, often from Wolbachia, can hold most of the bacterial reads, so the share of bacterial reads that is recognised can be higher or lower than the shares given here.

    What the set does not predict. The set tells whether an amplicon is bacterial, not how much bacterial DNA a sample will yield. That depends on primer mismatches, annealing temperature and how much target DNA the sample holds. Off-target amplification is usually much larger in eDNA, where animal DNA is scarce, than in bulk samples of animal tissue (Elbrecht et al. 2019; Collins et al. 2019).

    Possible improvements:

    • Tree placement. Placing ASVs that are far from both references on a reference tree, as DARN does, instead of a single identity comparison.
    • Protein-level comparison for very divergent ASVs, where nucleotide identity loses resolution.
    • Which bacteria each primer pair amplifies. A table of the bacterial lineages that common primer pairs can amplify, distributed with the release, for study design.
    • More genomes per species, for variation within species.
    • Genomes outside GTDB, with genes predicted by Pyrodigal (Larralde 2022). Pyrodigal is tested to give the same predictions as the current Prodigal code (v2.6.3+31b300a, which differs from the 2.6.3 release in a few edge cases) and runs faster.
    • A curated classifier for the oxidase families, to separate family B, family C and the nitric oxide reductases.
    • Sharing the MIDORI2 list with the MIDORI2 maintainers.
